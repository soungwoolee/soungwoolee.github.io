*Logical Writing*, *Fermi Estimation*, *Hypothesis Thinking*, and *Say It with Charts*

## I spent half a day on one blank slide

This was in my first year. I was asked to make one slide, and I stared at an empty screen for half a day. Nobody had scolded me, and the content wasn't hard. I just didn't know what to write. I had never thought about what a slide should say, or in what order to show it.

Consulting means giving companies the analysis and opinions they use to make decisions. But I had never properly learned analysis methods, how to form a hypothesis, how to estimate a number, or how to build a slide. Friends who had been in a business strategy club at university had this drilled into them. I wanted to catch up fast, so over two months starting in January 2023 I read four books back to back and wrote a review of each on my blog.

Now, five years in, I went back and reread those reviews. Some of it I used exactly as written. Some of it turned out much harder in practice than on the page.

## "Make it MECE" describes the result. The actual work is matching levels of abstraction

*Logical Writing* is where I first properly learned MECE: no overlaps, no gaps. It shows up everywhere, from setting a project's scope to outlining a report.

But "go make this MECE" is a slightly odd instruction. No overlaps and no gaps describes the state after you're done. It doesn't tell you how to get there. In practice, most mistakes are about level. Split industries into agriculture, mining, wholesale and retail, IT, and services, then slip interior design in among them, and it looks wrong right away. A concept from one level down got mixed in. That example is easy. On real projects, the levels are blurry far more often.

On a customer lifetime value project for an appliance maker, I spent a long time deciding how to group customers. People who bought through retail partners, people who bought on the company's own store, people who used its home appliance app, and people who used its smart TVs all overlapped, and some came before others. In the end, the grouping came from what we wanted to do with it. It could look complicated when laid out, but a good grouping had to be simple enough for the listener to get in one pass.

People who pay attention to this and people who don't end up very far apart. If you don't, you say something far more fundamentally wrong than a misaligned row on a slide. And someone who structures well is looking at the whole picture, so they don't get stuck in one corner and they prioritize better. I have a lot more to say about MECE, so I wrote it up separately.

## Estimation wasn't just for interviews

I thought *Fermi Estimation* was an interview prep book. Nobody at work asks you for "daily sales at the Starbucks by Yeouido station." But building up a number step by step is something I do almost every day. Forecasting future revenue from each customer's purchase history on the lifetime value project worked the same way. So did guessing, before looking, which accounts were behind most of a margin swing.

The habit that stuck the longest from this book is writing assumptions and facts down separately. There was no need to hide that an assumption existed. As long as the logic behind each calculation was written down, it was almost always fine.

## On charts, having a message matters more than where the title goes

*Say It with Charts* tells you to write message titles instead of topic titles: "A's revenue has grown for five years" instead of "A's revenue over five years." In practice it depends. Some clients find long titles slow them down before they ever reach the chart, and prefer a few keywords. That's a style choice. What didn't change is that the message has to be somewhere on the slide.

The hard part is pulling out the message. Looking at a plain chart and saying, in one sentence, what it means and why it happened. Five years in, it's still hard, and that's why it's fun.

## Open the data without a hypothesis and you sink

The book I learned most through experience is *Hypothesis Thinking*. Form a provisional answer first, then collect only what you need to test it. Three years ago I wrote in my review that "organizing data without a hypothesis feels like sinking into a swamp." I've sunk into that swamp many times since.

Early in the lifetime value project, I opened tables one after another with no goal. Without a hypothesis about what a customer would buy next, digging through every table was endlessly frustrating. A hypothesis like "customers who bought two or more TVs will also buy a large refrigerator" was what told me which tables to look at.

The same thing happened on an auto parts project. At first I just opened up each system to see what data it had, and it felt hopeless. Only after we set the hypothesis that "a handful of accounts will explain most of the margin swings" could I focus on the data that would test it. In the end, 10 of more than 300 accounts explained 70% of the margin variance.

It isn't only data. The more information you put on a slide, the harder it is to tie together, and the longer you talk, the weaker it gets. So these days, even when designing a data platform, I start by asking what problem it's meant to solve and what the business goal is.

## And now AI does these skills better than I do

Writing this, I realized AI now does much of what I learned over three years better than I do. Opening tables one by one, organizing numbers, drawing charts: AI is more thorough and faster. If I were starting my first year today, I wouldn't spend half a day on that slide. I'd ask AI for a first draft.

People are still better at forming hypotheses and judging levels of abstraction. But I think that's a matter of time. Not long ago, harness engineering, carefully building the frame AI works inside, was the hot topic. Then models got better at reading intent, and it mattered less almost overnight. Any single skill buys you less time than it used to.

## What's left is knowing what you're looking for

So are the four books useless now?

I couldn't write that slide for half a day not because I didn't know how to draw charts, but because I didn't know what I wanted to say, or to whom. A report isn't something you publish alone. It's closer to a letter to the person making the decision, so you start by deciding how you want them to react. That's what *Logical Writing* says from its first chapter.

Working with AI is the same. You have to tell it exactly what you want and keep it heading the same way. In the end, practice at holding onto the problem that leads to real action looks like the thing that will stay useful the longest.

The efficiency gap between people who use AI and people who don't is already huge. So I'm doing both: using AI well, and separately keeping my own judgment sharp. For that second part, these four books from my first year are still the most useful ones I have.

## If I were recommending them to a junior

1. *Hypothesis Thinking*: read it before you open any data
2. *Logical Writing*: when you're stuck in front of a blank slide
3. *Say It with Charts*: once you know your message
4. *Fermi Estimation*: when you need a feel for building numbers up
